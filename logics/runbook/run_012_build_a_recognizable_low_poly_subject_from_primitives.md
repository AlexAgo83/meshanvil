# Trigger
- A demonstration or fixture needs a recognizable subject (a vehicle, a figure) built from `bpy`/`bmesh` primitives rather than a downloaded or donated file -- no licence to verify, nothing to remove later.
- The generated subject looks wrong -- an unrecognizable blob, a hollow-looking limb, a flap poking past the silhouette -- and the fix has to be geometric, not a texture or shading trick a low-poly flat-shaded render has no room for.
- A ray-based measurement (thin-feature, visibility) on a generated subject reports a number that does not match what the generator's own comments claim it built.

# Prerequisites
- Render after every real change, not just after the script exits clean. A script that runs without error and reports a plausible vertex count still needs its own render checked by eye (`work/renders/*_3q.png` or equivalent) -- a wrong-looking result can run to completion silently.
- Know which reports are honest measurements and which are the generator's own intent. `tools/scan_defects.py`, `tools/voxel_remesh.py` etc. measure the file as it exists; a docstring only states what the author meant to build.

# Procedure
1. **Build a recognizable silhouette by sweeping cross-section rings along the main axis, not by deforming a single box.** A cube stretched and pinched (proportional-edit box modeling, the tutorial-standard approach) fights the tool at every step for an asymmetric profile like a car body. Defining N stations as explicit ring cross-sections (`[(x, -w, z0), ..., (x, w, z0)]`, one per station) and bridging consecutive rings into quads with `bmesh.ops` gives direct control over where the shape narrows, rises, and tapers, and is a handful of arithmetic lines rather than a modeling session. `tools/make_demo_vehicle.py`'s `ring()`/`stations` is this pattern; three earlier attempts (disconnected panels, a single dorsal profile with no side walls) were unrecognizable before this one held.
2. **Keep every panel narrower than the hull it is meant to sit inside of, checked at its own extremes, not just at its centre.** A flat accessory panel (skid plate, belly, roof rack) sized to a fixed fraction of the hull's *widest* station will be wider than the hull at any station it spans that tapers below that width -- it pokes out as a visible sliver past the silhouette there, worst at the narrow ends. Compute or eyeball the hull's width at the panel's own X-extremes before picking the panel's Y half-width, not just at the hull's widest point.
3. **A flat disk cap meets a sphere safely only at the sphere's own equator; anywhere else it leaves an arbitrarily thin sliver.** Two primitives joined without welding (the technique that keeps everything manifold by construction, see below) still touch as two separate surfaces at their contact; a torso cylinder's flat top embedded partway up a head sphere crosses that sphere off-equator, and the crossing is a sliver whose thickness depends on exactly where the disk sits -- not visible by eye at rest scale, but the exact kind of feature a ray-based thin-feature scanner is built to catch. The junction that is dead-on the sphere's own equator (a hip joining a torso's bottom, on this figure) has no such sliver and is safe to leave capped.
4. **Cap what is safe, leave open only the specific junction that is not -- do not blanket-uncap every part sharing a helper function.** The first fix for the sliver above removed end caps from every limb using the same helper, which also removed a visual cue (a capped end reads as a solid limb; an uncapped one renders as a dark hollow tube, obviously wrong at any zoom). Add an argument (`open_end="top"`) so the helper caps by default and only opens the one junction actually proven risky by step 3, rather than making every caller pay for one part's problem.
5. **Before trusting a ray-based thin-feature report, check whether it is measuring the feature or the gap next to it.** A ray-cast thickness scanner finds the nearest opposing surface along a cast direction; four fingers spaced closer than they are thick get measured as the *gap* between two fingers, which is a different (and differently thin) feature than the finger itself. Space repeated thin features apart by several times their own thickness (this figure uses `FINGER_R * 5.0`) so the scanner's nearest hit is guaranteed to be the feature's own opposite wall, not a neighbour's.
6. **Verify a claim the generator's comment makes against the real chain's own numbers, and soften the comment if the chain cannot back it.** "Mostly hidden" survived a session of widening a belly panel trying to reach "never visible" for an interior part; `ray_visibility`'s own grid never confirmed full concealment. The comment, the README, and the build script were all corrected to the claim the measured run actually supports, rather than left asserting a stronger one.
7. **An offset sized against one attachment point does not clear every attachment point sharing that offset, when the surface it has to clear varies between them.** A wheel's outward Y-offset was picked to clear the hull at the front wheel's station; the rear station is wider there (the hull tapers unevenly front to back), so the same offset left the rear wheel sitting partly inside the hull's side wall -- invisible as a bug from directly side-on (both wheels still read as circles) and obvious the moment the camera moves off-axis. Compute the offset against the widest station any instance of the part actually touches, not the first one checked.
8. **Under flat (no-material) shading, two faces sharing a normal render at the same brightness regardless of the depth between them -- a coplanar detail meant to be seen has to change the surface's angle, not just its distance.** A small disc added to make a wheel's flat end read as a hub rather than a bare pipe stub never appeared in any render, at any zoom, because its face pointed the same direction as the tyre's own face behind it; Workbench's flat lambert shading has no depth cue to draw between them. Confirmed by an isolated render of the wheel alone before spending more time on it. A bevel on the same edge worked immediately, because a bevel is a face at a different angle, which is the one thing flat shading actually responds to.
9. **The editable subject and the watertight solid are two artefacts, not two stages of one.** A generated sports car left its generator as 27 objects and 2,238 vertices -- one object per material group, the topology the stations were written to produce -- and came out of `solidify` + `voxel_remesh` as 1 object and 155,668 vertices. That derivative is the right input for a print check and the wrong thing to hand back to someone who asked for a model to edit: the component boundaries, the material groups and every intentional edge are gone, and none of the 153,000 new vertices carries shape information the 2,238 did not already carry. Read a vertex count as a cost, never as a quality. meshanvil makes this cheap to get right -- every operation writes a new file and never edits its input, so the master survives whatever the branch does -- but a chain script still has to *say* which artefact is the deliverable, or the last report in the list reads as the destination. `tools/build_sportscar_chain.sh` stops at the master and takes `--solid` to derive the print branch.
10. **Iterate the shape on the cheap representation, and derive the expensive one once.** The voxel branch above takes minutes and produces a 4.6 MB file; the generator takes under a second and produces 127 KB. Silhouette work that runs the full chain each time pays the second cost to learn nothing -- the remesh cannot tell you that a roofline is too far back. Get the shape right on the generated master, with renders, and run the validation branch when there is something worth validating.
11. **A green geometry check is not a verdict on the shape.** Manifold edge counts, shell counts and a script that exits 0 were all true of a version whose wheel arches sliced the tops off all four wheels, and of an earlier one that rendered as a doorstop. For anything judged by appearance -- vehicles, figures, products -- the loop is: generate, render front, rear, side and three-quarter, look at them, name the proportion that is wrong, change the generator, regenerate. Stopping at "the mesh is valid" is stopping before the work starts.
12. **Read the style off the application's own assets before inventing one.** A request for an asset "in the app's visual identity" is answerable from the repository: `viewer/icon.png` is a faceted low-poly anvil in graphite with its working surfaces in `--active` (#ed9e5c, the same accent `viewer/viewer.css` uses for the wordmark and the focus ring). That settles flat versus smooth shading, how wide a chamfer should be, which surfaces carry the accent colour, and how much detail belongs -- none of which is a matter of taste once the icon exists. A smoother, more photoreal hammer would have been a better hammer and a worse meshanvil asset.
13. **Two finishes on one forging are material slots, not two objects -- as soon as anything moves afterwards.** Splitting faces out of a loft so each is its own object works while the shared seam stays put; that is how the sports car's glasshouse is cut out of its body. A bevel moves things. Chamfering each part separately displaced the same seam twice, independently, and 64 edges survived the weld; restricting the bevel to interior edges did not help either, because an interior edge terminating on a boundary vertex still moves it. One closed mesh with a second material slot per face has no seam to tear, and `mesh.materials` plus `face.material_index` is the whole mechanism.
14. **A staged build is a chain of transforms, not a script that writes N files.** To make a model's construction reviewable stage by stage, write the generator as an operation whose input is the previous stage's output and whose job is to *add to what it receives*: `meshanvil run make_vise <file> --set stage=N`. The parent is then derived from the input's fingerprint like any other link, so the stages form one chain with before/after counts, a render per stage and a wipe between consecutive states -- and the viewer walks it with no special support. The alternative, a generator writing six .blend files beside the chain, produces six parentless reports: six chains, no lineage, and nothing to step through. `tools/make_vise.py` is the worked example; the seed is an empty scene so stage 1 has to build the blockout rather than inherit it, and the output name strips any stage suffix the input carried, so six stages give `vise_s1` to `vise_s6` rather than `vise_s1_s2_s3_s4_s5_s6`.
15. **A stage must be able to edit what earlier stages left, not only add to it.** "Refinement" that replaces the blockout with different geometry is not a stage, it is a second model wearing the first one's number, and the before/after counts stop meaning anything. The vise's refinement stage tapers and chamfers the parts already there: 12 objects in, 12 objects out, 280 vertices to 872. That number is the evidence the stage is real, and it is in the report whether anyone looks or not.

# Verification
- Re-run the full build chain (`build_demo_chain.sh` / `build_figurine_chain.sh` with no argument) after any generator change and diff the reported numbers against the previous run: vertex counts, `thin_feature_mm`, `thin_feature_voxels`, bbox drift, and which ops report `ok` vs `failed`. A script that still runs to completion is not proof the branch behaviour it exists to demonstrate is unchanged.
- Take a fresh 3q render after any geometry change and look at it. A vertex-count and bbox check that matches the previous run does not confirm a visual regression (open pipe ends, a sliver past the hull) is gone -- only the render does.
- Crop and zoom a suspected render artifact before concluding it is geometry. An anti-aliased ridge highlight where two faces meet at a shallow angle can read as a distinct pale sliver at thumbnail size and vanish under a zoomed crop of the same pixels.
- On a staged chain, read the object and vertex counts down the chain before looking at anything. A stage whose before and after are identical did nothing; a stage whose object count collapses replaced rather than refined.
- Look at the render at the size the asset will be used at. A butt swell and a waist sized to true proportions vanish at icon size; a mascot exaggerates both, and only the render says by how much.
- Compare the vertex count and the object count before and after any global remesh, and say what the difference bought. If the answer is "watertightness", that is a validation artefact; keep it and keep the master.
- Render the derivative from the same views as the master and put them side by side. On the sports car this is exactly what shows that the remesh bought a closed solid and cost every panel boundary.
- `grep` for a helper function's name after narrowing its use (`uncapped()` -> `half_capped()` for one junction only) to confirm nothing still calls the old blanket version.

# Rollback
- Nothing here writes outside `work/out/` and `work/demo/*` -- a bad generator change is fixed by editing the script and re-running it, not by reverting a destructive operation on a real file.

# Limits to state, not to hide
- These are the shapes this project's two subjects needed (a swept-ring car body, a sphere-and-cylinder figure); a different silhouette may need a different primitive-composition pattern entirely.
- Step 5's spacing rule assumes the scanner casts along a fixed direction set (this project's ray grid); a scanner casting only along the feature's own long axis would not have this failure mode.
- "Recognizable" was judged by eye against a real render, not against a quantitative silhouette metric -- there is no automated check here for "looks like a car."
